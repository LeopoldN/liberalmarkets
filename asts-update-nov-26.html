ASTS Quarterly Update: This Too Shall Pass

The market is pricing a delay as if it were a failure. Blacklift, while risky, is the right answer to AST’s biggest constraint: launch capacity.

By Nicholas Leopold 13 November 2026 13 min read

It’s currently 1 am here in Freiburg and I’m battling the flu, but there has been a 72% drop from the stock’s peak in May and a bunch of information in the last few months, so I feel I owe everyone a well-thought-out piece on the company.

My thesis in one sentence: the market is pricing a delay as if it were a failure, and Blacklift, while risky, is the right answer to AST’s biggest constraint, which is launch capacity.

First and foremost, the statement I get from every person I’ve spoken to about ASTS is “Wow, your cost basis is $15? You’re so lucky. I wish I got in that low.” Well… congratulations! You too can now lower your cost basis significantly. It’s striking how many people say they wish they could buy low, but when the stock actually falls in the short term, they get cold feet and run away. If you believed in the company at $100, you should at least re-examine it at $36.

Second note before I begin: I want to reiterate that Wall Street tends to anchor a stock’s price to 3–5-year forecasts of its earnings and growth. As we’ll get to later in this article, the company has been delayed… again, which is the main reason we saw a drop from $100+ to the $30s. Like always, I will say that if you want to make money in stocks, you invest with the goal of holding until the business matures. In this case, ASTS probably won’t mature until the mid to late 2030s. This puts the real value of the company too far out for most Wall Street forecasts. This short-term mindset of hedge funds creates opportunities like this latter half of this year to buy the stock at valuations that even I thought would never be possible.

Part 1The Cash Question

In December 2025, I wrote an email newsletter called “Martian Money and the SpaceXification of ASTS,” and it’s worth checking how it held up. In it, I wrote that the company could reach about $3.2 billion in cash given its at-the-market (ATM) stock sales and prepayments from mobile network operators (MNOs). At the end of Q3 2026, AST reported just over $3.2 billion in cash, cash equivalents and restricted cash. I then speculated about what the company was going to use it for, since it was well above what it needed to start launching satellites and generating revenue.

My first scenario was that AST and Blue Origin would work together and the cash would be used to finance Blue Origin launches exclusively (and potentially a buyout of AST SpaceMobile by Blue Origin). This became partly true, but as you’re all aware, Blue Origin’s New Glenn rocket exploded during a static-fire test on May 28, after an April flight left BlueBird 7 in too low an orbit, so I don’t see a future where AST gets acquired or relies entirely on Blue Origin.

The second scenario was a merger of equals between Rocket Lab and AST SpaceMobile. Its strategic logic rested almost entirely on launch access: Rocket Lab’s Neutron rocket would have given AST a dedicated path to orbit that it could not secure on its own. Blacklift removes that rationale. If AST builds its own vehicle, it no longer needs to merge with a rocket company to get launch capacity. Neutron’s own timetable also weakens the case: its first launch has slipped to 2027, so a merger would not have eased AST’s near-term launch constraint anyway.

My third scenario was that AST would use the $3.2 billion to buy Firefly, which at the time had a market cap of $3 billion and would guarantee launch capacity, faster cadence, cost control, and SpaceX-level vertical integration.

Now, was I right about any of these? Yes and no. Luckily, AST never merged with Blue Origin or Rocket Lab, but instead of acquiring Firefly, it agreed to buy Firefly’s engines and license the design to build our own rockets that would launch in Wallops flight facility.

In my December 2025 newsletter, I made a forecast for how many satellites would be in orbit. There were five at the time, and the guidance then pointed to about 16 by the end of Q1 2026. The table below shows how that target, and the company’s own, has moved since:

In under a year, the deadline for 45 satellites has moved from the end of 2026 to mid-2027. The year-end target of 45–60 will fall short by roughly 30 satellites.

Some of this was outside the company’s control. Blue Origin’s New Glenn rocket exploded during a test in May, BlueBird 7 was lost after an April launch left it in too low an orbit, and AST has had to wait for SpaceX launch slots. Some of it was self-inflicted: AST’s goal of building six satellites a month slipped from the first half of 2026 to December. Either way, the lesson is the same. As long as AST depends on other companies’ rockets, including a direct competitor’s, its timeline is not its own. That alone is enough reason to build its own launch capability, which brings us to Blacklift.

Part 3The Earnings Call

Q3 2026 by the numbers

  • Revenue: $42.3M, up 187% from a year earlier but below the ~$44.5M analysts expected. It came mostly from gateway hardware sales (the ground stations that link the satellites to carrier networks) and US government milestone payments. There’s no commercial service revenue yet.
  • Net loss to common shareholders: $132.4M for the quarter, $554.3M year to date.
  • Capital expenditures (capex), year to date: $1.15B, up 73% from a year earlier, as satellite production ramps up.
  • Full-year 2026 revenue guidance: $150–200M, reaffirmed. Hitting it requires roughly $62–112M in Q4, versus $42M this quarter.
  • Cash: ~$3.2B, including restricted cash.
  • How long does the cash last? Over the first nine months of 2026, AST spent about $1.15B on capex and about $315M running the business, roughly $490M a quarter. Blacklift adds a reported ~$400M up front plus about $40M a quarter in engine and engineering payments. At that pace, $3.2B covers roughly five to six quarters, into early or mid-2028. That’s before Blacklift’s first planned launch in late 2028. Japan’s $1B of non-dilutive funding (money that doesn’t require issuing new shares), government contracts and early service revenue extend the runway. Even so, you should expect AST to raise more capital before Blacklift flies.

The earnings call was less interesting than the Blacklift event the company livestreamed, so I’ll give a quick overview before continuing onto the juice.

First, management said it has signed launch agreements for more than 100 satellites to be deployed during 2027 and 2028, across multiple providers such as SpaceX, Blue Origin, United Launch Alliance (ULA), the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) and others. So while Blacklift is in development, the company should still be able to get the necessary satellites into orbit for global commercial service before 2029.

Second, the US government is using AST’s technology for space-based AMTI (air moving target indicator): using satellites to detect and track rockets, planes, drones, etc. globally for early warning and tracking systems. AST has won several US government awards, including over $100 million in funded near-term contracts announced this year, and secured about $1 billion in non-dilutive funding from Japan for J-LEO. Its total contracted backlog across carriers and governments is roughly $1.3 billion.

The funding is important, but the bigger deal is that AST secured a priority rating under the Defense Priorities and Allocations System (DPAS) for its dual-use government and commercial satellite program. I suspect this rating will push SpaceX and other US providers to prioritize these launches, since DPAS-rated orders take precedence over unrated ones. If so, AST could have 25 of the 45 satellites needed for continuous service by early 2027. I also expect that satellites 26-45 will be covered too, which would again push SpaceX and other US launch providers to prioritize them.

Third, management emphasized something I have been talking about for years. The current satellite architecture lets AST connect to far more than phones: Internet of Things (IoT) devices, space-based tracking, secure messaging, federal emergency services, sovereign country networks, missiles, drones, and robotics, even devices smaller than phones, so long as they have an antenna.

On the call, an analyst asked about AST’s relationships with Google and Meta, and the two are at very different stages. Google is an investor: it bought into AST’s convertible notes alongside AT&T and Vodafone in January 2024, and it’s working with AST to build satellite connectivity into Android phones. Meta is earlier. It isn’t an investor or a formal partner, but in August a senior Meta executive publicly described an “intensive technical and product workshop” with AST on bringing WhatsApp voice, video, messaging and Meta AI to AST’s network. That’s a meaningful signal, since WhatsApp has billions of users, but it isn’t a contract, and Meta is also working with Starlink in New Zealand. Management added that AST has other, undisclosed relationships with large companies in AI and robotics. Until those are named, I’m treating them as upside, not part of my base case.

I wouldn’t be surprised to see Amazon here too, for example connecting its delivery drones to AST’s satellites. The obvious objection is that Amazon is building its own constellation, Amazon Leo (formerly Project Kuiper). The case for AST would be that its satellites work with standard cellular radios, which are lighter and cheaper to put on a drone than a satellite dish.

Finally, management placed a heavy emphasis on national sovereignty. The example was J-LEO (Japan’s low Earth orbit satellite program), which I have written about ad nauseam before: a partnership between AST and Rakuten in Japan where the Japanese government retains full control over the data and use of the satellites. Japan’s Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications is providing about $1 billion of non-dilutive funding to build and launch the satellites, which connect through Rakuten’s network. This is the first time a national government has paid for the sovereign model, and offers a template that management intends to replicate elsewhere.

Europe is the obvious next candidate. AST’s joint venture with Vodafone is a natural vehicle for a European system that is not controlled by the US government.

Part 4The Livestream

This might be my favorite thing the company has done in a very long time. During a livestream, CEO Abel Avellan gave a presentation to a crowd of Wall Street investors. He showed live video of the factory and satellites being produced and even wore a leather jacket! Looking at you, Jensen Huang. First I will go through the CliffsNotes and a few fun facts, then I’ll categorize the bigger themes.

28 satellites are in final stages, and about 95% of each satellite is built in-house. Abel stated there is “hopefully a new one coming very soon” regarding US carrier partners and a “new one in Europe,” so in my mind this could mean that T-Mobile and its parent company, Deutsche Telekom, will partner with AST. With AT&T and Verizon already signed, that would give AST partnerships covering nearly all US phone subscribers.

Abel on Japan: “Our friends from Japan just gave us a billion dollars to build more of these [points to satellites] - arigatō gozaimasu” (thank you in Japanese)

At 28 satellites (around March/April 2027), AST plans to start beta service in the US, EU, Japan, Saudi Arabia, and Canada, so 28 is the next milestone to watch.

Satellite 28 is ready to go. Satellites 29-51 are in production.

Starting with BlueBird 34, AST will fit its satellites with a new ASIC (application-specific integrated circuit), a custom chip that handles the satellite’s signal processing. The company says it will begin full-scale ASIC integration with BlueBird 34. The chip offers up to 10 GHz of processing bandwidth per satellite and is expected to deliver a 10-fold throughput improvement compared with Block 1 satellites. These figures describe how much radio spectrum each satellite can process at once, not the frequencies it transmits on. According to Abel, that capacity has risen from about 100 MHz on the original satellites to about 1 GHz on today’s and will reach 10 GHz on the ASIC-equipped ones.

Partnerships are strategic assets. Unlike SpaceX, which may need to buy spectrum access for billions of dollars in each country it operates in, AST has partnerships with almost every large global MNO, which gives it fast, cheap access to each market’s spectrum.

AST is not building in the hope that people will buy; it is building against a demand backlog. It is supply-constrained. Demand for the satellites is massive across telecom companies, national governments (US, Japan, EU, Brazil, Canada, etc.), and militaries. Currently the US wants these satellites for dual-use military purposes, but it’s plausible that other countries will also pay for their own satellites to use in military operations.

To paraphrase Abel, no government (especially the US government) wants one company dominating space. The space market is too large and relevant to be controlled by one single company. The Japan formula will be applied worldwide to all different countries. Partner with an American company [AST] to deliver a satellite system they keep control of. Data and kill switches all stay within their countries. Other countries don’t have the engineers, launch capability, or technical know-how to build satellites like AST does, or a big enough market (population) to justify the tens of billions or more that it would take to build their own in-house system. Therefore, paying a billion dollars like Japan and getting control of your satellites, while knowing someone else (think Elon Musk and Starlink in Ukraine) can’t just turn them on and off, is a fundamentally good deal that he expects to be replicated many times over.

In the livestream, Abel expanded on the “larger than original market” plan I alluded to before. He said AST has MNO deals covering 3 billion subscribers, but is working on other methods that could unlock another 3 billion devices: any device - a streetlamp, a home meter, solar arrays, border drones/sensors, and hundreds of millions of other devices. The argument is that AST’s very large antennas in orbit can close the link with any normal cellular radio, with no special hardware on the ground. Whereas Starlink has shown that they can only work on newer phones or devices with tuned antennas. He also said that this is a “multi-trillion dollar opportunity”.

Part 5Blacklift

This is the big announcement of the week, and I have come around to it. As I’ve stated numerous times before, AST is launch-constrained, and I assumed it would either buy a company or spend a lot of its $3.2 billion on securing guaranteed launches. Blacklift is a middle ground. In total, the deal with Firefly is worth more than $1.3 billion in cash and shares and covers engines for the first three rockets, three years of engineering support and a perpetual license to manufacture the engines. Earlier this year, I was frustrated that the company raised billions through dilution without saying why. This looks like the reason, and if Blacklift works, it justifies those raises.

Blacklift is a 20-ton, three-stage rocket that will carry up to 5 satellites per launch and is targeted at 2 launches a month. That means capacity for 120 satellites a year while producing only 72 a year, which leads me to believe this is A) to work through the backlog and B) to eventually scale production from 6 to 10 a month. The reason AST is focused on a three-stage and not a two-stage rocket is that the first stage separates much earlier, at lower speed, which makes it easier to recover and reuse. Management believes reuse of the first stage is key to lowering the cost per launch.

During the presentation, Abel leaned heavily on the argument that AST is starting on third base: it doesn’t need to spend billions and wait through 10 years of testing to build the engines. The rocket is fully composite, and AST’s satellites are already composite and designed to fit Falcon 9s, so the company mainly needs to focus on designing the housing around the satellites and the fuel tanks.

The $1.3 billion to Firefly does not just buy the engines; it also buys the right to manufacture the engines in-house (lowering the cost per launch and moving toward full vertical integration), access to engineers at Firefly, its test tools, and its manufacturing capacity.

I’ll end this section with some statements Abel made during the livestream (paraphrased from my notes).

  • I’m not Musk and I don’t want to be. We’re fundamentally different in our relationships with MNOs and other partnerships. We partner; we don’t compete. We enhance; we don’t insult them.
  • I’m not doing this because we like rockets; we’re doing this because we want to accelerate our business plan and make money faster.
  • We don’t want to be in the launch business; we just have too much demand and not enough ability to launch.
  • We’re done waiting for other people’s rockets; we’ll build it ourselves.

Conclusion

After a day to digest everything that has happened, I feel even more confident in this company. Yes, the risk increased; yes, the timeline has been pushed again and again. But past delays are sunk costs; the only question that matters is what the business is worth from here.
